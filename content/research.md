I direct the [**Vanderbilt Initiative for Probes of Extreme Relativity (VIPER)**](https://as.vanderbilt.edu/viper/), an intensely collaborative research group investigating gravitational waves across the nanohertz–millihertz frequency band. VIPER develops astrostatistics methods, Bayesian parameter estimation tools, AI-driven data analysis pipelines, and large-scale simulations to probe the universe's most extreme environments: supermassive black holes, relativistic binaries, neutron stars, and early-Universe processes.
{.lead}

## Nanohertz gravitational waves {#nanohertz}

{{< img src="images/pta_champion.jpg" alt="Illustration of a pulsar timing array: Earth at the center of a distorted spacetime grid, with lines to pulsars around it" caption="A pulsar timing array: gravitational waves passing between Earth and the pulsars perturb the arrival times of their pulses." >}}

The main thrust of my research is **searching for nanohertz gravitational waves** from distant sources using a web of Milky Way pulsars (a Pulsar Timing Array), and unveiling the properties of the supermassive black holes, cosmic strings, or early Universe processes that produce these GWs.

**Pulsars** are rapidly rotating, highly magnetized neutron stars that emit beams of radiation along their magnetic field axis. Millisecond pulsars (**MSPs**) spin hundreds of times a second with extraordinarily stable pulse arrival times, making them natural clocks in the sky.

> **June 29, 2023** — I announced NANOGrav's evidence for a cosmic background of nanohertz-frequency gravitational waves at NSF Headquarters, alongside Kip Thorne and Jocelyn Bell Burnell.

I have developed many of the signal models, statistical techniques, and coding infrastructure underlying the global pulsar timing array effort — including Bayesian spectral characterization, open-source analysis pipelines, forward models for population forecasting, and host-galaxy identification frameworks for supermassive black hole binaries.

## Supermassive black-hole binaries {#binaries}

The gravitational-wave background is expected to be dominated by the combined signal of a cosmic population of supermassive black-hole binaries. My group forecasts when pulsar timing arrays will resolve the first individual binaries, develops targeted searches for candidates identified electromagnetically, and builds frameworks for identifying the host galaxies of binaries found in gravitational waves — connecting gravitational-wave detections to the galaxies and quasars around them.

## LISA and the millihertz band {#lisa}

As one of six US scientists appointed to the **ESA–NASA LISA Science Team**, I am developing population-inference methods for LISA sources within the global fit, targeting stellar-mass binaries, massive black-hole mergers, and extreme mass-ratio inspirals across the millihertz band.

LISA — three spacecraft exchanging laser beams across 2.5-million-kilometer arms — was adopted by ESA in 2024 and is planned for launch in the 2030s.

## Methods and software {#methods}

The common thread is statistical: turning noisy, heterogeneous data into reliable astrophysical inference. My group's work includes Bayesian spectral characterization of the gravitational-wave background, Gibbs-sampling schemes for full PTA analyses, generalized optimal statistics for mapping the background across the sky and spectrum, and normalizing flows and neural emulators for fast inference. Our code is open source — see [github.com/stevertaylor](https://github.com/stevertaylor).
